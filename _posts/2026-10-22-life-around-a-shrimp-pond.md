Stand near a shrimp pond long enough and you start telling time differently. Not by the clock on your phone, but by the feeding round, the color of the water, and whether the aerators are running. I spent time around one, and the season left a mark on me that office work never did.

## A season has its own calendar

A shrimp-farming season does not start when the shrimp arrive. It starts with an empty pond: drained, sun-dried, cleaned, limed, refilled with brackish water, and left to settle until the water turns the right shade of green. Only then do the postlarvae go in — tiny, nearly transparent, and impossibly numerous.

From there the calendar is roughly fixed. The first weeks are about nursing the young stock along: small frequent feeds, gentle aeration, constant watching. Then comes the long middle stretch, the grow-out months, when the shrimp put on size and the work settles into its daily rhythm. At the end comes the harvest — sometimes partial, thinning the pond in rounds, sometimes a full drain-down — and then the pond rests, and everything starts over.

Nobody around the pond talks about this in weeks and milestones. They talk about it the way farmers everywhere talk: this pond is early, that pond is late, the water is young, the crop is nearly done. The season is a story with a beginning, a middle, and an ending everybody can read at a glance — except me, at first. I had to learn to see it.

## Mornings belong to the pond

The working day starts early, before the heat. The first job is the feeding round: walking the pond banks with feed, broadcasting it in arcs across the water, checking the feeding trays that tell you how much of yesterday's feed actually got eaten. An empty tray means the shrimp are hungry and growing; a full one means something is off, and you adjust.

Then comes the water check. You look at the color — green is usually good, too dark or too clear tells its own story — you note the smell, the surface film, how the shrimp behave near the banks. Experienced farmers read a pond the way a mechanic listens to an engine: nothing mystical, just thousands of repetitions compressed into a glance.

The equipment needs tending too. Paddlewheel aerators churn the water to keep oxygen up, especially at night and in the hot months; pumps and sluice gates move water in and out on schedule. None of it is complicated machinery, but all of it has to work, every day, because the pond does not pause for breakdowns.

Evenings mirror the mornings in reverse: a last feed before dark, the aerators switched on for the night, a final walk around the banks with a lamp. Some nights someone sleeps near the pond — during delicate stretches of the season, or when the weather turns, the pond gets a night watch. I found that touching: an animal you can barely see, in water you cannot look through, guarded like treasure while the village sleeps.

## Water and weather decide almost everything

Here is the part that humbled me. You can do everything right — feed on time, keep the gear running, watch the trays — and still lose the season to things nobody controls. A week of heavy rain drops the salinity and stresses the stock. A heatwave sucks the oxygen out of the water overnight. Runoff from nearby fields brings whatever it brings. And disease moves through a farming region the way flu moves through a school: fast, indifferent, and largely unstoppable once it starts.

So a lot of life around the pond is really life around uncertainty. People watch the sky the way city people watch the stock ticker. Conversations return to the same questions: will it rain tonight, is the wind shifting, how is the neighbour's pond doing. There is a whole vocabulary of euphemism for trouble — nobody says the worst word out loud while the season is still running.

What struck me is the absence of complaining. Not stoicism as performance — just the practical recognition that complaining changes nothing, while an extra night check might. Worry gets converted into work. That conversion, repeated daily for months, is the real texture of the season: low-grade, intelligent vigilance, without drama and without end.

## The price question hangs over everything

Even a good harvest is only half the story. Shrimp are sold into a market whose price moves with harvest gluts across the region, export demand, and the size grade of your own crop — bigger shrimp fetch more per kilo, so the decision of when to harvest is itself a gamble. Sell early and safe, or hold on for size and risk the weather turning while you wait.

I watched people do this arithmetic in their heads constantly: feed costs sunk so far, days left in the season, what the buyers were offering last week, what the ponds two villages over were pulling out. Nobody has perfect information. Everybody decides anyway, because the pond will not wait for certainty.

There is something clarifying about work where the accounts close in public. In my line of work, a mediocre quarter can be narrated away; around the pond, the harvest is the harvest, weighed in front of everyone. People carry that honesty into everything else. I trusted pond-side judgments about completely unrelated matters more than most expert opinions I have paid for.

## What being around it taught me

The first lesson was attention. I arrived with the standard city habit of partial presence — body in one place, mind on the phone. The pond punishes that. The signs are subtle and cumulative: a tray left uneaten two days running, water a shade off, shrimp jumping differently at dusk. Miss the small things and you meet the big thing unprepared. I started putting the phone away without being asked. It felt less like discipline and more like catching up with everyone else.

The second lesson was patience with processes you cannot hurry. You cannot grow shrimp faster by wanting it. The season takes what it takes, and fretting compresses nothing. Software taught me that everything can be optimized; the pond taught me that some things can only be tended. There is a difference, and I had mostly forgotten it.

The third was respect for manual, repetitive work. Feeding rounds, tray checks, bank walks — done thousands of times, each time as if it mattered, because each time it does. No novelty, no leverage, no audience. Just care, applied evenly, for months. I had absorbed the idea that repetition is what you automate away. Around the pond I saw repetition as the work itself, done by people who understand something I am still learning.

## Why it matters to me

I did not grow up expecting a shrimp pond to be part of my inner landscape. But seasons have a way of attaching themselves to you: the smell of brackish water at dawn, the churn of the aerators at night, the particular quiet of the banks when the feed is done and there is nothing to do but watch. I carry those sensory details the way other people carry songs from a certain year.

It matters because it reconnected me with a tempo I come from and had drifted away from. Most of my days now run on notifications and deadlines — fast, interruptible, abstract. Pond time is slow, continuous, and concrete: living things growing at their own speed whether or not anyone is watching. Remembering that such a tempo exists, and that people I care about still live inside it, keeps me honest about what my own hurry is for.

If you ever get the chance to spend a season — or even a few weeks — around a working pond, take it. Go before dawn at least once. Walk the banks after the evening feed. Ask what the water is saying and listen to the answer longer than feels necessary. You will not come back with a technique or a takeaway you can put on a slide. You will come back with a slower eye and a recalibrated sense of what a day's work can be. For me, that was more than enough.
