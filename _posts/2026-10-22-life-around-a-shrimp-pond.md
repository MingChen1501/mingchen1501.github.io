Stand near a shrimp pond long enough and you start telling time differently. Not by the clock on your phone, but by the feeding round, the color of the water, and whether the aerators are running. I spent time around one, and the season left a mark on me that office work never did.

The pond I came to know is an ordinary working pond in a coastal farming area — one stretch of brackish water among many, and what happens around it happens around countless ponds just like it.

## A season has its own calendar

A shrimp-farming season starts not with the shrimp but with an empty pond: drained, sun-dried, limed, refilled, and left to settle until the water turns the right shade of green. Only then do the postlarvae go in — tiny, nearly transparent, impossibly numerous.

From there the calendar is roughly fixed: early weeks nursing the young stock — small frequent feeds, gentle aeration, constant watching — then the long middle stretch, when the shrimp put on size and the work settles into rhythm. At the end comes the harvest — sometimes partial, thinning the pond in rounds, sometimes a full drain-down — and then the pond rests, and everything starts over.

Nobody around the pond talks in weeks and milestones: this pond is early, that pond is late, the water is young, the crop is nearly done. The season is a story everybody can read at a glance — except me, at first.

## Mornings belong to the pond

The working day starts early, before the heat, with the feeding round: walking the banks, broadcasting feed in arcs, checking the trays that tell you how much of yesterday's feed got eaten. An empty tray means the shrimp are hungry and growing; a full one means something is off.

Then comes the water check. You look at the color — green is usually good, too dark or too clear tells its own story — you note the smell, the surface film, how the shrimp behave near the banks. Experienced farmers read a pond the way a mechanic listens to an engine: nothing mystical, just thousands of repetitions compressed into a glance.

The equipment needs tending too: paddlewheel aerators keeping oxygen up through night and heat, pumps and sluice gates moving water on schedule. None of it is complicated, but all of it has to work every day, because the pond does not pause for breakdowns.

I never learned to size a pond in hectares or name its stock, and I won't pretend otherwise here. What I remember is simpler: water that changed color with the sky, and banks worn smooth from daily rounds.

Evenings mirror the mornings: a last feed before dark, aerators on for the night, a final walk with a lamp. Some nights someone sleeps near the pond — in delicate stretches, or when the weather turns, the pond gets a night watch. An animal you can barely see, in water you cannot look through, guarded like treasure while the village sleeps.

## Water and weather decide almost everything

Here is the part that humbled me. You can do everything right and still lose the season to things nobody controls: a week of rain dropping salinity, a heatwave sucking oxygen out overnight, runoff bringing whatever it brings, disease moving through a farming region the way flu moves through a school.

So life around the pond is really life around uncertainty. People watch the sky the way city people watch the stock ticker, and nobody says the worst word out loud while the season is still running.

What struck me is the absence of complaining: not stoicism, just the practical recognition that complaining changes nothing while an extra night check might. Worry gets converted into work — repeated daily for months, the real texture of the season.

## The price question hangs over everything

Even a good harvest is only half the story: shrimp prices move with regional gluts, export demand, and the size grade of your own crop — bigger shrimp fetch more per kilo, so deciding when to harvest is itself a gamble. Sell early and safe, or hold on for size and risk the weather turning.

I watched people do this arithmetic constantly: feed costs so far, days left, last week's buyer prices, what neighbouring ponds were pulling out. Nobody has perfect information, but everybody decides anyway — the pond will not wait for certainty.

Around the pond, past seasons sort into three kinds — good ones, bad ones, ordinary ones — recounted in the same even tone, as weather rather than verdict. What counts is not the outcome but what it teaches about the water, and whether anyone watched closely enough to learn.

There is something clarifying about work where the accounts close in public: a mediocre quarter can be narrated away, but the harvest is the harvest, weighed in front of everyone. People carry that honesty into everything else — I trusted pond-side judgments on unrelated matters more than most expert opinions I have paid for.

## What being around it taught me

The first lesson was attention. I arrived with the standard city habit of partial presence — body in one place, mind on the phone. The pond punishes that. The signs are subtle and cumulative: a tray left uneaten two days running, water a shade off. Miss the small things and you meet the big thing unprepared. I started putting the phone away without being asked.

The second lesson was patience with processes you cannot hurry: the season takes what it takes, and fretting compresses nothing. Software taught me everything can be optimized; the pond taught me some things can only be tended.

The third was respect for manual, repetitive work: feeding rounds, tray checks, bank walks — done thousands of times, each time as if it mattered, because each time it does. Around the pond I saw repetition as the work itself, done by people who understand something I am still learning.

No one sat me down to teach me to read the water. Lessons arrive unannounced: a glance at the surface, a feed ration adjusted without comment, and you learn by standing nearby long enough. Nothing is explained; everything is absorbed.

## Why it matters to me

I did not grow up expecting a shrimp pond to be part of my inner landscape. But seasons have a way of attaching themselves to you: brackish water at dawn, aerators churning at night, the particular quiet of the banks when the feed is done. I carry those details the way other people carry songs from a certain year.

![Morning sun breaking through scattered clouds above red clay tile rooftops, with a silhouetted palm tree and treeline below](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/7a23d529-7a4b-419b-87b3-596dc86010cd){: width="960" height="1280" }

It matters because it reconnected me with a tempo I come from and had drifted from. My days run on notifications and deadlines — fast, interruptible, abstract. Pond time is slow and concrete: living things growing at their own speed, watched or not. Remembering that such a tempo exists, and that people still live inside it, keeps me honest about what my own hurry is for.

My connection to the pond today is the simplest kind: I no longer keep its hours, but I keep its tempo in mind. The next season will begin as seasons there always begin — an empty pond, a stretch of waiting, and the same quiet bet that attention is enough.

If you ever get the chance to spend time around a working pond, take it. Go before dawn at least once; walk the banks after the evening feed. Ask what the water is saying and listen longer than feels necessary. You will come back with a slower eye and a recalibrated sense of what a day's work can be.
